# Method & sources — why JourneyMap is built the way it is

*This is the canonical text behind the in-app **Method** page. Every choice is either drawn from established practice (marked "canonical") or an explicit, labelled adaptation of ours ("our adaptation"). Nothing is presented as doctrine that isn't.*

## What this is
JourneyMap maps how a supporter moves through one of your fundraising programs — annual giving, a signature event, planned giving — **stage by stage and lane by lane**. It is a working document, not a static deliverable: it first describes the experience as it is today, then collects the ideas and numbers for where it should go.

## Where it comes from — three pillars of established practice

1. **Journey mapping & service design (canonical).**
   The map's anatomy — stages × layers; the donor-experience band; touchpoints; the frontstage/backstage split — comes from the customer-journey-mapping and service-blueprinting canon:
   - James Kalbach, *Mapping Experiences* (O'Reilly, 2016) — the foundational textbook of journey/experience maps and blueprints.
   - Nielsen Norman Group — *Service Blueprints* and *Customer Journey Maps* (nngroup.com).
   - G. Lynn Shostack, "Designing Services That Deliver" (*Harvard Business Review*, 1984) — the origin of service blueprinting, whose layers ours compress into four.

2. **Donor-centred fundraising (canonical).**
   Penelope Burk, *Donor-Centered Fundraising* (Cygnus, 2003; updated 2018) — the research-based classic: donors stay when thanked **promptly, specifically, informatively**; needs are met *before* the next ask. This drives what the **Donor experience** lane asks and the template emphasis on thank-you speed and stewardship.

3. **Relationship fundraising & moves management (canonical).**
   - Ken Burnett, *Relationship Fundraising* (White Lion Press, 1992; 2nd ed. Wiley, 2002).
   - **Moves management** — developed by David Dunlop at Cornell ("changing people's attitudes so they want to give"): identify → qualify → cultivate → solicit → steward; institutionalised in Raiser's Edge and comparable CRMs. It underlies our **Leadership giving** stage axis and the no-ask cultivation cadence.

## How to read a map
| Element | What it means |
|---|---|
| Stage columns | Lifecycle phases, left → right (awareness → first gift → retention → growth → drift → lapse; or an event/program lifecycle). |
| Lanes | Four layers per stage: **Donor experience** (what they think/feel/decide), **Channels & touchpoints**, **Org actions** (backstage work), **Gaps & opportunities**. Each lane carries a guiding question. |
| Recurring episodes lane *(our adaptation)* | The appeal/event calendar mapped onto the stages it serves — fundraising runs on cycles, and this lane makes the rhythm visible inside the relationship. |
| Cards | **Current** state (solid) or **💡 Idea** (dashed, priority dot). Click to edit; drag to move. |
| Metric chips *(our adaptation)* | *current → target (suggested norm)*, attached to the card that claims it, rolled up under each stage's header. |

## Why these choices
The ideas layer restates the classic **as-is / to-be** distinction — standard in journey-mapping practice (Kalbach) — in one artifact: a single map holds today's reality and the path forward.

## Where the approach tries to improve on practice (our adaptations, being tested)
- **As-is and to-be in one place.** Most maps stop at describing today; this one deliberately collects the ideas for tomorrow alongside it, with priorities.
- **Numbers without a homework lane.** Staff fill the *current*, the tool suggests the *benchmark*, leaders set the *target* — nobody fills a spreadsheet row of guesses.
- **Questions, not blank space.** Each lane asks a question (standard workshop-facilitation practice in journey mapping; wording is ours), so filling a map is guided rather than daunting.
- **The calendar made visible.** The recurring-episodes lane is our fundraising-specific extension of the generic anatomy.

## Honesty before confidence
- **Benchmark numbers are suggested, not authoritative** — confirm them before presenting (the editor labels them so).
- A journey map **complements data, it never replaces it**. The map answers "what happens and how it feels"; the CRM answers "how much and how fast".
- Maps describe the **common path** — a relationship hypothesis, not a fact about any one donor.
